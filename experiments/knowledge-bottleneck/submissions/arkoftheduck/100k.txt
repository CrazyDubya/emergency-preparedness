ARKOFTHEDUCK FIELD MANUAL (100K TIER)
Plain-language operating manual for ordinary people during severe, prolonged
infrastructure failure. This is preparedness guidance, NOT a substitute for
professionals. Whenever real emergency services, clinicians, or official
instructions are available, they take priority over this document.

=== HOW TO USE THIS ===
Skim the INDEX. In a crisis, jump to the numbered section you need. Each
section leads with the single most important action. When a detail is
context-sensitive and could be dangerous if wrong, this manual gives a
principle and a "when to escalate" rule instead of false precision.

=== 1. MINDSET AND THE ORDER OF SURVIVAL ===
Panic kills. Stop, breathe, look, plan, act. Adopt "protect life first, then
capability." Work the ORDER OF LIFE and never skip up the list to comfort while
a higher threat is unmet:
- Air: minutes. Blocked airway, smoke, toxic gas, drowning.
- Severe bleeding and body-core temperature: minutes to an hour.
- Water: about 3 days without.
- Food: about 3 weeks without.
- Morale/social order: degrades over days and can kill through bad decisions.
Assign roles early (water, security, medical, sanitation, comms). Keep the
group together, warm, dry, hydrated, informed. Rest and ration effort; injuries
and mistakes rise with exhaustion. Write things down; memory fails under stress.

=== 2. IMMEDIATE SCENE SAFETY ===
Do not become the next casualty. Before helping others or entering a structure:
- Look up, down, around: fire, smoke, downed power lines, gas smell, unstable
 walls/roof, rising or moving water, sharp debris, traffic.
- If you smell gas, hear hissing, or see leaning structures or floodwater
 rising: leave now, move uphill/upwind, do not use switches, flames, or engines.
- Turn off utilities if you know how and it is safe: gas at the meter (only if
 you smell gas; needs a wrench; once off, a pro must turn it back on),
 electricity at the main breaker, water at the main valve.
- Assume downed lines are live. Stay back at least the length of a bus. If a
 line touches your car, stay inside unless fire forces you out; then jump clear
 landing with feet together, and shuffle away without touching car and ground
 at once.

=== 3. WATER ===
You need roughly 2-4 liters per person per day for drinking, more for heat,
work, illness, or nursing. Dehydration degrades judgment fast.
FIND: stored water, water heater tank, toilet tank (not bowl; not if chemical
tabs used), pipes, canned vegetable liquid, rain, dew, streams, ponds, snow
(melt before eating - eating snow chills you and wastes energy).
MAKE IT SAFE - two steps:
1) CLEAR it: let sediment settle, then pour through a clean cloth or sand/cloth
 filter. Clearer water disinfects better.
2) DISINFECT (kills germs; clearing alone does not):
 - BOIL (best): bring to a rolling boil 1 minute; 3 minutes above ~2000 m
 (6500 ft). Cover, cool, store.
 - BLEACH: plain unscented household bleach (~5-6% sodium hypochlorite). Add
 about 2 drops per liter (8 drops per gallon), double if cloudy/cold, stir,
 wait 30 minutes; a faint chlorine smell should remain. If not, repeat once.
 - SOLAR (SODIS): clear PET bottle, clear water, full sun 6 hours (2 days if
 cloudy). Last resort.
 - Commercial filters/tablets: follow their label.
NOTE: Boiling and bleach kill microbes but do NOT remove chemical/fuel/heavy
contamination. If water may be chemically polluted (industrial, floodwater
through chemicals), do not drink it; find another source. Distillation removes
salt and many chemicals but not all; it is a last resort for salt/brackish water.
STORE: clean, food-safe, covered containers; keep off the floor; label; rotate.

=== 4. FOOD ===
Ration from day one; you rarely know how long. Eat perishable/fridge food
first, then freezer, then shelf-stable. A fridge holds ~4 h closed; a full
freezer ~48 h. "When in doubt, throw it out" - but do not waste safe food.
SAFETY: discard food that touched floodwater, is warm and was previously
refrigerated for >2 h, smells or looks wrong, or is in swollen/leaking/rusted
cans. You cannot see or smell all toxins; err toward caution with meat, dairy,
eggs, cooked rice.
NUTRITION: prioritize calories and salts when active or hot. Do not force food
if water is scarce - digestion uses water. Sprouting seeds/beans adds vitamins.
INFANTS: breastfeeding is safest in crisis; if using formula, use safe water.

=== 5. WARMTH, COLD, HEAT, SHELTER ===
Core temperature is a life threat. Wet + wind + still air steal heat fastest.
COLD: stay DRY; wet clothing loses most of its insulation. Layer: wicking base,
insulating mid, wind/water shell. Cover head, neck, hands, feet. Insulate from
the ground (that is where you lose heat) with anything: cardboard, leaves,
packs. Make shelter small so your body heat fills it. Share warmth. Move to
generate heat but do not sweat.
HYPOTHERMIA signs: intense shivering, then clumsiness, confusion, slurred
speech, then shivering STOPS (danger). Warm gently: dry them, insulate, skin-to-
skin, warm sweet drinks if fully alert. Handle gently; do not rub limbs.
HEAT: rest in shade, loosen clothing, wet skin and fan, drink water with a
little salt/food. HEATSTROKE (hot skin, confusion, collapse, may stop sweating)
is deadly: cool aggressively NOW - water, wet cloths, fan, shade, immerse if
possible - and get help.
SHELTER PRIORITIES: dry, out of wind, insulated from ground, ventilated (see
carbon monoxide), defensible from water/fire, near water and signaling.

=== 6. AIR, CARBON MONOXIDE, FIRE ===
CARBON MONOXIDE (CO) is the most common post-disaster killer after the event.
It is invisible and odorless. NEVER run generators, engines, charcoal, gas
grills, camp stoves, or unvented fuel heaters inside a home, tent, garage, or
near windows. Generators go OUTSIDE, far from and downwind of openings.
Symptoms: headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness, confusion; multiple people or
pets sick at once. Response: get everyone to fresh air immediately, then seek
help. A battery CO alarm is cheap lifesaving gear.
FIRE - prevent and fight small, flee big:
- Keep flames away from fuel, fabric, and sleeping people. Never leave open
 flame unattended.
- Small fire: smother (lid, dirt, blanket), or use an extinguisher with PASS -
 Pull pin, Aim at base, Squeeze, Sweep. Grease fire: cover, cut heat, never
 water. Electrical: cut power, do not use water.
- If it is spreading or filling a room with smoke, GET OUT and stay out. Crawl
 low under smoke. Feel doors with the back of your hand; if hot, use another
 way. Have two exits from every space in your plan.
STARTING FIRE: gather tinder (dry, fluffy), kindling (pencil to thumb), fuel
(bigger). Shelter the flame from wind, build small, add air, feed gradually.
Keep a fire small, contained, and downwind of your shelter.

=== 7. FIRST AID: LIFE THREATS FIRST ===
Principle: fix what kills fastest, in order, and get to professional care as
soon as possible. This manual gives principles and escalation, not doses.
CHECK: Are they responsive? Breathing? Bleeding badly? Approach only if the
scene is safe.
NOT BREATHING / NO PULSE: start CPR. Push hard and fast in the center of the
chest, about 100-120/min, ~5-6 cm deep, let it recoil; minimize pauses. If
trained and willing, add rescue breaths; if not, hands-only is far better than
nothing. Use an AED if available and follow its voice.
CHOKING (cannot cough/speak/breathe): 5 firm back blows between shoulder blades,
then 5 abdominal thrusts (inward-upward above the navel); alternate. Infant: 5
back blows then 5 chest thrusts, head low. If they collapse, start CPR.
SEVERE BLEEDING: press hard directly on the wound with cloth; keep pressing
(10+ min) and add layers, do not peek. If a limb bleeds uncontrollably, apply a
tourniquet 5-7 cm above the wound (not on a joint), tighten until bleeding
stops, note the time, do not loosen. Pack deep wounds with cloth and press.
SHOCK (pale, cold, clammy, fast weak pulse, confusion): lay flat, keep warm,
reassure, treat causes (bleeding), sips of water only if fully alert and no
belly injury. Escalate.
BURNS: stop the burning; cool with clean cool (not ice) water 20 min; remove
non-stuck jewelry/clothing; cover loosely clean/non-fluffy. Do not pop blisters
or apply grease. Large, deep, airway, face, hands, genitals, or electrical/
chemical burns: escalate urgently. Chemical: brush off dry powder, flush with
water long.
FRACTURES/SPRAINS: do not straighten obvious deformities; immobilize/splint in
found position padding above and below the joint; check the limb stays warm and
pink (circulation). Open fracture: cover wound, control bleeding, escalate.
HEAD/SPINE injury: if suspected, keep them still, support the head in line, do
not move unless in danger; watch breathing.
UNCONSCIOUS but breathing: place on their side (recovery position) to protect
the airway; monitor.
MEDICATIONS: prefer principle over precise dosing you are unsure of. Do not
guess unfamiliar drug doses. Keep chronic medicines going if you can; ration and
protect insulin/inhalers/heart/seizure meds; seek resupply early. For pain/fever
use only medicines the person already tolerates, at labeled adult/child doses;
never exceed the label; avoid giving aspirin to children. When unsure, wait and
escalate rather than risk harm.
WOUND CARE over days: clean hands, irrigate with safe water, keep covered and
dry, watch for infection (spreading redness, heat, pus, red streaks, fever) -
infection is the slow killer; escalate for antibiotics if these appear.

=== 8. ILLNESS, HYGIENE, SANITATION ===
More people die after disasters from disease and dirty water than from the
event. Hygiene is survival.
HANDS: wash with soap and safe water before food/medical and after toilet/waste;
use ash or sanitizer if no soap.
HUMAN WASTE: keep it far (at least ~30 m / 100 ft and downhill) from water
sources, kitchens, and living space. Options: existing toilet with manual
flush (pour water), bucket toilet with lid and bags plus cover material
(sawdust, soil, lime) to control smell and flies, or a cathole 15-20 cm deep
buried after. Never contaminate drinking water.
DIARRHEA/VOMITING is dangerous through dehydration, especially for kids and
elders. Treat with frequent small sips of oral rehydration: roughly 6 level
teaspoons sugar + half a level teaspoon salt in 1 liter safe water; keep giving.
Escalate for bloody stool, high fever, severe dehydration (sunken eyes, no
urine, lethargy).
GARBAGE and bodies: separate and cover waste; manage the deceased with respect,
away from water and food, documented, until authorities can respond.
PESTS: control flies and mosquitoes (cover water, drain standing water, screens)
- they spread disease.

=== 9. POWER, LIGHT, FUEL ===
Conserve first. Turn off nonessential loads. Protect the ability to charge a
phone/radio.
PHONE: dim screen, airplane mode when no signal, close apps, keep it warm, one
device on at a time; text uses less power/bandwidth than calls.
BATTERIES: keep spares; store cool; do not mix old/new or types; watch for
leaks. Car battery + inverter can run small loads; do not drain the starting
battery flat.
GENERATORS: OUTSIDE only (see CO). Never backfeed into house wiring without a
proper transfer switch - it can kill line workers and you. Refuel cold/off.
FUEL: store approved containers, ventilated, away from living space and
ignition; label and date; stabilize gasoline; keep clear of heat.
LIGHT: headlamps free hands; ration; candles are a fire risk - never leave lit
or near fabric/sleep.

=== 10. COMMUNICATIONS AND SIGNALING ===
Information and coordination save lives; so does being found.
MONITOR: a battery/hand-crank/solar radio for official broadcasts (AM/FM,
NOAA weather radio in the US). Follow official evacuation and boil-water orders.
FAMILY PLAN: agree in advance on an out-of-area contact (often reachable when
local lines are jammed), two meeting points (near home, outside neighborhood),
and that TEXT often works when calls fail. Write numbers on paper.
TWO-WAY RADIO: FRS/GMRS/CB for short range; set a channel and check-in times to
save battery. Ham radio reaches far if licensed operators are present.
SIGNAL FOR RESCUE: THREE of anything = distress (3 whistle blasts, 3 fires in a
triangle, 3 flashes). A whistle beats your voice and lasts longer. Use a mirror/
shiny object to flash aircraft. Make large ground signals with contrast: V =
need assistance, X = need medical help, arrow = direction traveled. Keep a
signal ready; you may get one chance.

=== 11. STAY OR GO: EVACUATION AND NAVIGATION ===
DECIDE deliberately. STAY if your shelter is safe, you have water/warmth, and
leaving is more dangerous (e.g., told to shelter in place, or roads are deadly).
GO if there is fire, flood, structural, gas, or violence threat, if officials
order evacuation, or if staying means running out of water/heat with no resupply.
BEFORE LEAVING: grab your go-bag, wear sturdy shoes and layers, fill water, tell
someone your route and destination, leave a note (who, where, when, condition),
shut off utilities if appropriate, take documents and medicines.
ROUTES: prefer official routes; avoid flooded roads - "Turn Around, Don't Drown":
even 15 cm of moving water can knock you down and 30 cm can float a car. Avoid
low ground in floods, high open ground in lightning, and downwind of fire/chem.
NAVIGATION: keep a paper map and compass. Sun rises roughly east, sets west; at
solar noon shadows point away from the sun (toward true north in the northern
hemisphere, south in the southern). Note landmarks; mark your trail; travel in
daylight; do not travel exhausted or in whiteout.
VEHICLE: keep the tank at least half full pre-crisis; carry water, blankets,
jumper cables, basic tools; if stranded in cold, run engine only briefly and
only with a clear exhaust and a cracked window (CO), stay with the vehicle - it
is shelter and easier to find.

=== 12. PEOPLE: GROUPS, SECURITY, VULNERABLE MEMBERS ===
Cooperation outperforms isolation. Organize: shared tasks, clear decisions,
fair rationing, and watch shifts. Calm leadership and routine reduce panic.
SECURITY: lock/reinforce as reasonable; keep low profile (light/noise/odor
discipline) if others may be hostile; avoid displaying supplies. De-escalate
conflict; distance and negotiation beat confrontation. Protect children from
hazards and from wandering.
VULNERABLE MEMBERS: plan for infants (warmth, feeding, safe water), elders and
the ill (medications, mobility, temperature), people with disabilities (assistive
devices, communication needs), and pets (water, restraint, waste). Keep families
together; assign a buddy to each dependent person.
MENTAL HEALTH: expect fear, grief, and stress reactions; they are normal.
Maintain routines, talk, share tasks, rest, and watch for despair or reckless
behavior. Purpose and connection keep people alive.

=== 13. DOCUMENTS, MONEY, RECORDS ===
Keep waterproof copies (paper and/or offline digital) of ID, insurance, medical
info and medication lists, contacts, property and financial records, and
photos of home/valuables. Keep some cash in small bills - electronic payment
may be down. Record decisions, injuries, and who went where; good records speed
recovery, reunification, and aid/insurance claims.

=== 14. SCENARIO QUICK-ACTIONS ===
EARTHQUAKE: Drop, Cover, Hold On under sturdy furniture; stay away from windows;
if in bed, stay and cover your head; do not run outside during shaking. After:
check for injuries/gas/fire, expect aftershocks, get to open ground if the
building is damaged.
FIRE (structure/wildfire): get out and stay out; crawl low under smoke; wildfire
- evacuate early, close windows, remove flammables near the house, wear
non-synthetic clothing, go upwind/uphill away from the fire's path.
FLOOD: move to higher ground before water rises; never walk/drive through
floodwater; assume it is contaminated and electrified; boil-water rules after.
TORNADO: go to a small interior room/basement on the lowest floor, cover your
head and neck; a vehicle/mobile home is unsafe - get to sturdy shelter or lie
in a low ditch as last resort.
HURRICANE/CYCLONE: prepare days ahead, evacuate if told, shelter interior away
from windows during the storm, beware the calm eye, expect flooding and long
outages after.
WINTER STORM: stay in, conserve heat to one room, prevent CO, layer, keep pipes
from freezing (drip), watch for hypothermia.
EXTREME HEAT: stay cool, hydrate, never leave people/pets in vehicles, check on
elders, reduce activity to early/late hours.
POWER OUTAGE: conserve phone, keep fridge/freezer closed, no generators indoors,
watch for CO and food spoilage, check on neighbors.
PANDEMIC/DISEASE: distance, hygiene, ventilate, isolate the sick, protect the
vulnerable, follow official guidance.
CHEMICAL/HAZMAT: move upwind and uphill, get inside and seal (shelter-in-place)
or evacuate as ordered, remove and bag contaminated clothing, rinse skin/eyes
with water.
NUCLEAR/RADIATION or "dirty" event: get inside a sturdy building, go to the
center/basement, stay for at least 24 h unless told otherwise (Get Inside, Stay
Inside, Stay Tuned), remove outer clothing and wash to remove fallout.

=== 15. LONGER-TERM RECOVERY ===
When immediate threats pass, shift from surviving to sustaining.
WATER: secure a renewable source (rain catchment off clean roofs, protected
well/spring) plus ongoing disinfection and safe storage.
FOOD: transition to production - garden from saved seed, preserve harvests
(drying, salting, fermenting, canning if equipped), and protect stores from
pests and damp. Learn a few reliable local calorie crops.
SHELTER/ENERGY: weatherproof and repair; add insulation; develop safe heating/
cooking with proper ventilation; small solar/wind if available.
HEALTH: maintain sanitation discipline, wound care, and medication supply;
build a simple sick-care routine and isolation plan.
COMMUNITY: pool skills and labor, trade fairly, share knowledge, care for the
vulnerable, and rebuild governance and routine. Skills, tools, seeds, and
trust are the real long-term wealth. Teach others; redundancy of knowledge is
survival.

=== 16. KITS AND CHECKLISTS ===
GO-BAG (per person, grab-and-run): water + filter/tablets, high-calorie food,
weather-appropriate clothing and rain shell, emergency blanket, sturdy shoes,
flashlight/headlamp + batteries, whistle, multitool/knife, lighter/matches +
tinder, first-aid basics, personal medications, N95/dust mask, gloves, phone +
backup battery, paper map, cash, copies of documents, hygiene items, duct tape,
cordage, and a written contact/plan card.
HOME (shelter-in-place): 2 weeks water (or the means to disinfect), 2+ weeks
food, sanitation supplies (bags, cover material, lime), battery/crank radio,
CO alarm, fire extinguisher, extended first aid and medications, tools to shut
off utilities, lighting and fuel stored safely, warm bedding.
SKILLS beat gear: water disinfection, fire, first aid/CPR, navigation, basic
repair, food preservation, and calm decision-making. Practice before you need
them. Review and rotate supplies on a schedule.

REMEMBER: protect life first, then capability. Fix the fastest killer in front
of you, avoid becoming a casualty, keep your people together, and get to real
help as soon as it exists. Three of anything means help.

[src: power_and_energy/power_and_energy_guide.md]
This guide covers the essentials of backup power solutions and managing your energy consumption during a power outage.

[src: power_and_energy/power_and_energy_guide.md]
Having a way to generate your own electricity is a major advantage in a disaster. Here are the most common options, from simplest to most complex.

[src: power_and_energy/power_and_energy_guide.md]
* **What they are:** Small, portable batteries for charging phones, tablets, and other USB-powered devices.
* **Pros:** Inexpensive, easy to use, portable.
* **Cons:** Limited capacity. They won't power large appliances.
* **Best For:** Keeping your communication devices charged.

[src: power_and_energy/power_and_energy_guide.md]
### Tier 2: Portable Power Stations (Solar Generators)

[src: power_and_energy/power_and_energy_guide.md]
* **What they are:** Large, rechargeable battery packs with multiple outlets (AC, DC, USB). They can be recharged via a wall outlet, a car, or solar panels.
* **Pros:** Silent, no fumes (safe for indoor use), multiple charging options.
* **Cons:** More expensive than power banks, limited capacity (you can't run a refrigerator for days on end).
* **Best For:** Running small, essential devices like a laptop, a CPAP machine, or a few lights.

[src: power_and_energy/power_and_energy_guide.md]
* **What they are:** The traditional solution for backup power.
* **Pros:** Powerful (can run large appliances like refrigerators and freezers), relatively inexpensive for the amount of power they provide.
* **Cons:** **NEVER USE INDOORS.** They produce deadly carbon monoxide. They are loud, require a steady supply of gasoline (which can be hard to find in a disaster), and require regular maintenance.
* **Best For:** Powering essential large appliances.

[src: power_and_energy/power_and_energy_guide.md]
### Tier 4: Whole-Home Generators / Solar with Battery Backup

[src: power_and_energy/power_and_energy_guide.md]
* **What they are:** Professionally installed systems that can power your entire home.
* **Pros:** Seamless, automatic power. High capacity.
* **Cons:** Very expensive.
* **Best For:** Long-term, whole-home power solutions.

[src: power_and_energy/power_and_energy_guide.md]
During an outage, you need to be strategic about your energy use. Your goal is to make your stored power last as long as possible.

[src: power_and_energy/power_and_energy_guide.md]
* **Know Your Needs:** Before a disaster, figure out which of your devices are essential. Make a list of their power requirements (in watts).
* **Turn Things Off:** Unplug any device you are not actively using. Even in standby mode, many electronics draw a small amount of power (this is called a "phantom load").
* **Use Manual Alternatives:** Use a hand-crank can opener instead of an electric one. Use a manual coffee grinder. Look for non-electric alternatives for your daily tasks.
* **Lighting:** Use flashlights, headlamps, or solar-powered lanterns for light. Avoid using the lights in your home, as they are a significant power drain.
* **Heating and Cooling:** Your home's heating and cooling systems are likely your biggest energy consumers. Do not attempt to run them on a portable generator. For warmth, focus on heating a single, small room and use blankets and warm clothing. For cooling, use passive methods like opening windows at night and closing them during the day.

[src: power_and_energy/power_and_energy_guide.md]
By having a modest backup power solution and being mindful of your energy consumption, you can maintain a degree of normalcy and safety during a power outage.

[src: barter_and_trade/barter_and_trade_guide.md]
When the cash economy fails, commerce doesn't stop. It reverts to its oldest form: barter. This guide covers the basics of a barter economy and how to trade effectively.

[src: barter_and_trade/barter_and_trade_guide.md]
Not all items are created equal. In a post-disaster economy, value is determined by immediate need and scarcity.

[src: barter_and_trade/barter_and_trade_guide.md]
### Tier 1: The Essentials (The "Vices")

[src: barter_and_trade/barter_and_trade_guide.md]
These are small, highly desirable items that are consumed and have an immediate impact on morale and comfort. They are the most liquid and widely accepted barter items.

[src: barter_and_trade/barter_and_trade_guide.md]
* **Caffeine:** Instant coffee, tea bags.
* **Nicotine:** Cigarettes, cigars, vape juice.
* **Alcohol:** Small, sealed bottles of liquor (like airline bottles).
* **Sugar:** Hard candy, chocolate.

[src: barter_and_trade/barter_and_trade_guide.md]
### Tier 2: Consumables & Practical Items

[src: barter_and_trade/barter_and_trade_guide.md]
These are items that people will always need.

[src: barter_and_trade/barter_and_trade_guide.md]
* **Food:** Canned goods, rice, beans, flour.
* **Water Purification:** Bleach, purification tablets, spare filters.
* **Hygiene:** Soap, toothpaste, toilet paper, feminine hygiene products.
* **Fuel:** Propane canisters, butane, lamp oil, firewood.
* **Batteries:** All common sizes (AA, AAA, C, D).
* **First Aid:** Bandages, antiseptic wipes, pain relievers.

[src: barter_and_trade/barter_and_trade_guide.md]
Your knowledge and abilities are some of the most valuable things you can trade.

[src: barter_and_trade/barter_and_trade_guide.md]
* **Medical:** First aid, dental care.
* **Mechanical:** Small engine repair, vehicle maintenance.
* **Security:** Standing watch, security consulting.
* **Technical:** Radio repair, gunsmithing.
* **Domestic:** Sewing, cooking, childcare.

[src: barter_and_trade/barter_and_trade_guide.md]
These are less liquid, but can be traded for high-value returns.

[src: barter_and_trade/barter_and_trade_guide.md]
* **Precious Metals:** Gold and silver may retain value, but they are not useful for immediate needs.
* **Tools:** A good set of tools will always be valuable.
* **Weapons & Ammunition:** These have obvious value, but trading them carries significant risk.

[src: barter_and_trade/barter_and_trade_guide.md]
* **Start Small:** Don't start with large, high-value trades. Build trust with small transactions first.
* **Don't Trade at Home:** If possible, meet in a neutral, public location.
* **Bring a Friend:** There is safety in numbers. Never trade alone.
* **Don't Flash Your Stash:** Never reveal the full extent of your supplies. Only bring what you are willing to trade.
* **Inspect the Goods:** Make sure you are getting what you bargained for before you complete the trade.
* **Know When to Walk Away:** If a trade feels wrong, it probably is. Trust your gut and walk away.

By understanding what is valuable and how to trade safely, you can navigate a barter economy and get the supplies you need.

This guide covers the most critical first steps for securing your home and preventing further damage after a disaster.

[src: home_repair/home_repair_guide.md]
## 1. Safety First: When in Doubt, Get Out

[src: home_repair/home_repair_guide.md]
Before you even think about repairs, assess the structural safety of your home.

[src: home_repair/home_repair_guide.md]
* **Look for:**
 * Large cracks in the foundation or walls.
 * Sagging ceilings or roof.
 * The smell of gas or burning insulation.
* If you see any of these signs, **do not enter**. Have the home inspected by a professional when it is safe to do so.

[src: home_repair/home_repair_guide.md]
Knowing how to shut off your utilities is one of the most important post-disaster skills.

[src: home_repair/home_repair_guide.md]
* **When to Shut it Off:** If you see sparks, frayed wires, the smell of burning insulation, or if your home has been flooded.
* **How to Shut it Off:**
 1. Locate your main electrical panel (usually in the garage, basement, or an outside closet).
 2. Turn off all the individual circuit breakers first.
 3. Flip the main breaker (the large one at the top) to the "Off" position.

[src: home_repair/home_repair_guide.md]
* **When to Shut it Off:** **Only if you smell gas, hear a hissing sound, or see a broken gas line.**
* **How to Shut it Off:**
 1. Locate your gas meter on the exterior of your house.
 2. Use a crescent wrench or a specialized gas shut-off tool to turn the valve a quarter turn until it is perpendicular to the pipe.
 3. **NEVER** attempt to turn the gas back on yourself. This must be done by a qualified professional.

[src: home_repair/home_repair_guide.md]
* **When to Shut it Off:** If you have a burst pipe or if local authorities have issued a boil water alert.
* **How to Shut it Off:**
 1. Locate your home's main water shut-off valve. This is usually where the main water pipe enters your house, often in a basement or on an exterior wall.
 2. Turn the valve clockwise to close it.

[src: home_repair/home_repair_guide.md]
* **Board Up Windows:** Use plywood or other sturdy material to cover broken windows. This will protect your home from the elements and from intruders.
* **Tarp Your Roof:** If your roof is damaged, a securely fastened tarp can prevent water from getting into your home.
* **Document Everything:** Before you make any repairs, take photos and videos of all the damage for your insurance claim.

[src: home_repair/home_repair_guide.md]
This guide covers the immediate, critical steps. For more complex repairs, always consult a professional when it is safe to do so.

[src: psychology_and_community/psychology_and_community_guide.md]
## 1. The Psychology of Survival: The Rule of Threes

[src: psychology_and_community/psychology_and_community_guide.md]
The most important survival tool is your mind. A common saying in survival training is the "Rule of Threes":

[src: psychology_and_community/psychology_and_community_guide.md]
* You can survive **3 minutes** without air.
* You can survive **3 hours** without shelter in a harsh environment.
* You can survive **3 days** without water.
* You can survive **3 weeks** without food.

[src: psychology_and_community/psychology_and_community_guide.md]
This rule highlights the priorities. But underlying all of it is the will to live. Without a positive mental attitude and the will to survive, all the skills and supplies in the world are useless.

[src: psychology_and_community/psychology_and_community_guide.md]
* **Acceptance:** Accept the reality of the situation. Denial is a dangerous waste of time.
* **Positivity:** Maintain a positive but realistic outlook. Hope is a powerful motivator.
* **Focus:** Concentrate on what you can do *right now* to improve your situation. Don't get lost in long-term worries.
* **Calmness:** Panic leads to bad decisions. When you feel overwhelmed, stop, take a deep breath, and think before you act.

[src: psychology_and_community/psychology_and_community_guide.md]
In a long-term urban disaster, the lone wolf is a myth. The single most important factor for survival is a strong, cohesive group. A Mutual Assistance Group (MAG) is a pre-planned community of like-minded individuals who agree to pool their resources and skills in an emergency.

[src: psychology_and_community/psychology_and_community_guide.md]
* **Security:** There is safety in numbers. A group is a much harder target for desperate people.
* **Shared Resources:** No one person can have all the skills and supplies needed. A group allows for specialization (e.g., one person is a medic, another is a mechanic, another is a gardener).
* **Shared Workload:** Many hands make light work. A group can share the burden of tasks like standing watch, purifying water, and caring for the sick.
* **Psychological Support:** A group provides a sense of belonging and shared purpose, which is critical for long-term morale.

[src: psychology_and_community/psychology_and_community_guide.md]
1. **Start with Your Inner Circle:** Begin with trusted family and close friends.
2. **Expand to Your Neighborhood:** Get to know your neighbors *before* a disaster. Identify those who are reliable and have useful skills.
3. **Discuss Expectations:** Have an open and honest conversation about what each member is expected to contribute. This includes skills, supplies, and time.
4. **Create a Plan:** Develop a simple, written plan that outlines your group's goals, communication methods, and meeting places.
5. **Practice Together:** Conduct drills and practice your plan. This will build trust and identify weaknesses in your plan before a real emergency.

[src: psychology_and_community/psychology_and_community_guide.md]
Building a strong community is the ultimate preparedness strategy. Start today by getting to know your neighbors.

## 1. Family Emergency Communication Plan

[src: communications/communications_guide.md]
A simple, clear plan is essential for reconnecting with loved ones.

[src: communications/communications_guide.md]
* **Contact Information:**
 * Create a physical, waterproof list of all important phone numbers. Don't rely on your phone's contact list.
 * Include an **Out-of-Town Contact**. It is often easier to make long-distance calls than local ones during a regional disaster. This person can act as a central point of contact for your family.
* **Meeting Places:** Designate three specific locations:
 1. **Neighborhood Meeting Place:** A safe spot near your home (e.g., a specific tree, a neighbor's porch).
 2. **Regional Meeting Place:** A location outside your immediate neighborhood, in case you can't get home (e.g., a library, a friend's house).
 3. **Out-of-Town Meeting Place:** A location in another city or state, in case of a large-scale evacuation.
* **Communication Methods:**
 * **Text Messages First:** Text messages (SMS) often get through when voice calls fail because they use less network bandwidth.
 * **If You Must Call:** Keep calls brief to conserve your phone's battery and free up the network for others.
* **Practice the Plan:** Run through your plan with your family. Make sure everyone knows the meeting places and has the contact information.

[src: communications/communications_guide.md]
**Action Item:** Fill out a family communication plan. You can find excellent, free templates online from sources like Ready.gov.

[src: communications/communications_guide.md]
When cell phones and the internet are down, radios are your best bet for staying in touch.

[src: communications/communications_guide.md]
* **FRS (Family Radio Service):**
 * **License:** None required.
 * **Power:** Low power (max 2 watts).
 * **Range:** Short-range (typically 0.5 to 2 miles).
 * **Best For:** Communicating with family members within a small area (e.g., your neighborhood).
 * **Notes:** These are the common "walkie-talkies" you can buy in most stores. They are simple to use and a great starting point.

[src: communications/communications_guide.md]
* **GMRS (General Mobile Radio Service):**
 * **License:** Required (one license covers your entire immediate family, no test required).
 * **Power:** Higher power (up to 50 watts).
 * **Range:** Medium-range (typically 5 to 25 miles).
 * **Best For:** Communicating over longer distances, like across a town.
 * **Notes:** GMRS radios can use repeaters, which can significantly extend their range.

[src: communications/communications_guide.md]
* **Ham Radio (Amateur Radio):**
 * **License:** Required (must pass a test).
 * **Power:** High power.
 * **Range:** Long-range (can be global).
 * **Best For:** Communicating over very long distances, and for hobbyists who want to be part of a larger community.
 * **Notes:** Ham radio is the most powerful and versatile option, but it also has the highest barrier to entry.

[src: communications/communications_guide.md]
* **Listen First:** Before you transmit, listen to make sure the channel is clear.
* **Keep it Brief:** Be concise and to the point.
* **Identify Yourself:** Use your call sign (for GMRS and Ham) or a nickname.
* **Speak Clearly:** Use plain language. Avoid jargon.

[src: communications/communications_guide.md]
By having a solid family communication plan and a set of two-way radios, you can dramatically improve your ability to stay connected and informed during an emergency.

## The Core Four: Knots for Joining and Securing

[src: advanced_skills/essential_knots_guide.md]
These are the foundational knots that you will use most often.

[src: advanced_skills/essential_knots_guide.md]
1. **Bowline:**
 * **What it does:** Creates a fixed loop at the end of a rope that will not slip or tighten.
 * **Primary Use:** Rescue. You can tie it around yourself or someone else for hoisting to safety.
 * **Mnemonic:** "The rabbit comes out of the hole, around the tree, and back into the hole."

[src: advanced_skills/essential_knots_guide.md]
2. **Clove Hitch:**
 * **What it does:** Secures a rope to a post, tree, or other object.
 * **Primary Use:** Quickly attaching a rope to something. Useful for setting up shelters or securing gear.

[src: advanced_skills/essential_knots_guide.md]
3. **Sheet Bend:**
 * **What it does:** Joins two ropes together, even if they are of different thicknesses.
 * **Primary Use:** Making a longer rope from two shorter ones.

[src: advanced_skills/essential_knots_guide.md]
4. **Figure-Eight Follow Through:**
 * **What it does:** Creates a very strong, secure loop at the end of a rope.
 * **Primary Use:** Climbing and rescue. It is the standard knot for tying into a harness.

[src: advanced_skills/essential_knots_guide.md]
These knots have more specific, but very important, uses.

[src: advanced_skills/essential_knots_guide.md]
* **Double Fisherman's Knot:**
 * **What it does:** A very strong knot for joining two ropes of similar size.
 * **Primary Use:** Creating a rope loop (prussik loop) or for situations where the knot must not fail.

[src: advanced_skills/essential_knots_guide.md]
* **Prusik Knot:**
 * **What it does:** A friction hitch that can grip a rope. It will slide when not weighted, but will grip tightly when a load is applied.
 * **Primary Use:** Ascending a rope, or as a "progress capture" device in a hauling system.

[src: advanced_skills/essential_knots_guide.md]
* **Water Knot (Tape Knot):**
 * **What it does:** Joins two pieces of flat webbing together.
 * **Primary Use:** Creating slings or anchor points from webbing.

[src: advanced_skills/essential_knots_guide.md]

[src: security/security_guide.md]
This guide provides actionable steps to enhance your home security and personal safety before, during, and after a disaster.

[src: security/security_guide.md]
## 1. Home Security: Hardening Your Castle

[src: security/security_guide.md]
Your goal is to make your home a less attractive target and more resilient to damage.

[src: security/security_guide.md]
* **Reinforce Entry Points:**
 * **Doors:** Install heavy-duty deadbolts. Use longer screws (3 inches) for the strike plates so they anchor into the door frame itself, not just the trim.
 * **Windows:** Apply security film to your windows. This makes them harder to break and can prevent shattering. For ground-floor windows, consider installing locks or grilles.
 * **Sliding Doors:** Place a strong bar (like a cut broom handle) in the track to prevent it from being forced open.
* **Control Your Environment:**
 * **Lighting:** Install motion-sensor lights around the exterior of your home.
 * **Landscaping:** Trim bushes and trees near windows and doors to eliminate hiding spots.
 * **Fencing:** A good fence is a significant deterrent.
* **Know Your Utilities:**
 * Know the location of your main shut-offs for gas, water, and electricity.
 * Keep the necessary tools (like a wrench for the gas meter) permanently stored next to the shut-offs.

[src: security/security_guide.md]
* **Shelter in Place:** If you are staying home, stay in an interior room away from windows.
* **Low Profile:** Keep lights low and noise to a minimum, especially at night. You don't want to advertise that you have power or supplies when others don't.
* **Evacuation:** If you must evacuate, lock your home. Turn off utilities only if you suspect damage or if instructed to by authorities.

[src: security/security_guide.md]
## 2. Personal Safety & Situational Awareness

[src: security/security_guide.md]
Your most important safety tool is your brain. Pay attention to your surroundings.

[src: security/security_guide.md]
* **Avoidance:** The best way to win a dangerous encounter is to not be in it in the first place. If a situation feels wrong, it probably is. Leave immediately.
* **Gray Man Theory:** In a chaotic situation, your goal is to be uninteresting and unmemorable. Avoid wearing expensive clothes or flashy gear. Blend in.
* **Trust Your Gut:** Intuition is your subconscious mind recognizing that something is wrong. Don't ignore that feeling.
* **Travel in Groups:** There is safety in numbers. Avoid traveling alone, especially at night.

[src: security/security_guide.md]
* **Plan Your Routes:** Know multiple ways to get to and from important locations. If one route is blocked, you need an alternative.
* **Vary Your Routine:** Don't be predictable. If you follow the same route at the same time every day, you make yourself an easy target.
* **Stay Informed:** Use a battery-powered or hand-crank radio to monitor local news and emergency broadcasts.
* **De-escalation:** If you are confronted, try to de-escalate the situation verbally. Comply with demands if your life is at risk. Your possessions are not worth your life.

[src: security/security_guide.md]
By taking these steps, you can significantly improve your security and safety in a chaotic urban environment.

### **Radiation Units**
| Unit | Measures | Conversion |
|------|----------|------------|
| **CPM** | Counts per minute | Detector clicks |
| **mR/hr** | Milliroentgen per hour | Exposure rate |
| **Sv/hr** | Microsievert per hour | Dose rate |
| **mSv** | Millisievert | Cumulative dose |

[src: modern_threats/nuclear/radiation_detection_equipment.md]
#### **Conversion Factors**
- 1 mR/hr 10 Sv/hr (rough conversion)
- 1000 Sv = 1 mSv
- Background: 10-20 Sv/hr normal
- Concern level: 100+ Sv/hr

[src: modern_threats/nuclear/radiation_detection_equipment.md]
### **Action Levels**
| Reading | Action Required |
|---------|----------------|
| **0-50 Sv/hr** | Normal background |
| **50-100 Sv/hr** | Monitor, investigate source |
| **100-1000 Sv/hr** | Limit time, increase distance |
| **1000+ Sv/hr** | Evacuate area immediately |
| **10,000+ Sv/hr** | Extreme danger - flee |

[src: modern_threats/nuclear/radiation_detection_equipment.md]
### **Before First Use**
1. **Read manual thoroughly** - Each detector is different
2. **Install fresh batteries** - Check voltage requirements
3. **Perform background check** - Establish normal readings
4. **Test with check source** - Verify proper operation
5. **Practice operation** - Become familiar with controls

[src: modern_threats/nuclear/radiation_detection_equipment.md]
### **Taking Readings**
1. **Turn on detector** - Allow warm-up time if required
2. **Check battery** - Low battery gives false readings
3. **Hold properly** - Chest height, steady position
4. **Wait for stability** - Readings may fluctuate initially
5. **Record results** - Date, time, location, reading

[src: modern_threats/nuclear/radiation_detection_equipment.md]
### **Contamination Checking**
- **1 inch from surface** - Standard measurement distance
- **Systematic survey** - Cover all surfaces methodically
- **Compare to background** - 2x background = possible contamination
- **Multiple readings** - Confirm elevated readings
- **Mark contaminated areas** - Prevent spread

[src: modern_threats/nuclear/radiation_detection_equipment.md]

Before emergency, measure:
- [ ] Indoor background levels
- [ ] Outdoor background levels
- [ ] Different rooms and areas
- [ ] Seasonal variations
- [ ] Equipment check readings

# Faraday Cage Construction Guide - EMP Protection

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
## Overview
A Faraday cage is a metallic enclosure that protects electronic equipment from electromagnetic pulses (EMP) and solar flares by redirecting electrical energy around the contents rather than through them.

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
### **Option 1: Metal Trash Can (45 dB protection)**
**Cost: ~$50 | Time: 1 hour | Difficulty: 2/5**

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
#### Materials Needed:
- [ ] 31-gallon galvanized steel trash can (NOT aluminum)
- [ ] Aluminum HVAC tape (not regular duct tape)
- [ ] Cardboard or thick paper
- [ ] Foam padding
- [ ] Multimeter for testing

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
#### Construction Steps:
1. **Test the can** - Use magnet to verify it's steel
2. **Clean thoroughly** - Remove all paint, rust, labels
3. **Line interior** - Cardboard prevents metal contact with electronics
4. **Add padding** - Foam protects from physical damage
5. **Seal lid gaps** - Apply aluminum tape around rim when closing
6. **Test effectiveness** - AM radio should go silent inside

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
### **Option 2: Ammo Can (50 dB protection)**
**Cost: ~$30 | Time: 30 minutes | Difficulty: 1/5**

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
#### Materials Needed:
- [ ] .50 cal metal ammo can
- [ ] Conductive foam padding
- [ ] Rubber gasket (if missing)
- [ ] Anti-static bags

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
#### Construction Steps:
1. **Verify seal** - Should be watertight when closed
2. **Add conductive foam** - Lines interior surfaces
3. **Create compartments** - Organize with cardboard dividers
4. **Wrap items** - Use anti-static bags for extra protection
5. **Test seal** - Cell phone inside should lose all bars

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
### **Option 3: Room-Sized Cage (60+ dB protection)**
**Cost: ~$800 | Time: 2-3 days | Difficulty: 5/5**

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
#### Materials Needed:
- [ ] Copper mesh screen (6 feet wide)
- [ ] Conductive copper tape (2-inch wide)
- [ ] Aluminum foil (heavy duty)
- [ ] Copper grounding wire
- [ ] Conductive caulk

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
#### Construction Steps:
1. **Plan the room** - Interior room with fewest penetrations
2. **Install mesh** - Cover all surfaces with 6-inch overlap
3. **Seal seams** - Use conductive tape or solder joints
4. **Address penetrations** - Seal electrical outlets, pipes
5. **Create door seal** - Conductive gaskets around door frame
6. **Ground the cage** - Connect to building ground system

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
### **Attenuation Levels (dB)**
- **20 dB** = 99% reduction (basic protection)
- **40 dB** = 99.99% reduction (good protection)
- **60 dB** = 99.9999% reduction (excellent protection)
- **80+ dB** = Military-grade protection

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
### **Material Effectiveness**
| Material | Thickness | Attenuation |
|----------|-----------|-------------|
| Aluminum foil | 0.02mm | 20-30 dB |
| Steel can | 1mm | 40-50 dB |
| Copper mesh | Fine | 30-60 dB |
| Aluminum screen | Standard | 20-40 dB |

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
### **Critical Requirements**
1. **Continuous conductive path** - No gaps > 1/10 wavelength
2. **Metal-to-metal contact** - Paint/rust breaks continuity
3. **Proper sealing** - Openings are weak points
4. **Electrical isolation** - Contents don't touch cage walls

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
### **Common Mistakes to Avoid**
- Using aluminum trash cans (too thin)
- Leaving gaps in coverage
- Items touching cage walls directly
- Using regular duct tape instead of conductive tape
- Not testing effectiveness before storing items

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]

### **Priority 2 - Power Systems**

### **Priority 3 - Computing**

- [ ] E-readers with manuals

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]

### **Radio Test Method**
1. **Tune AM radio** to static between stations
2. **Place radio inside** cage and seal
3. **Static should disappear** completely
4. **Cell phone test** - Should show "No Service"
5. **Bluetooth test** - Should not connect to devices outside

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
### **Professional Testing**
- **Field strength meter** - Measures actual attenuation
- **Spectrum analyzer** - Tests across frequencies
- **Signal generator** - Provides known test signal

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
### **EMP Threats**
- **Solar flares** - 8-18 hours warning possible
- **Nuclear EMP** - No warning, instant effect
- **Local EMP** - Terrorist device, limited area

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
### **Protection Limitations**
- **Cage must be closed** - Open cage provides no protection
- **Size matters** - Larger openings = less protection
- **Frequency dependent** - Lower frequencies harder to stop
- **Not 100%** - Very powerful EMPs may still cause damage

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
### **Storage Tips**
- **Rotate inventory** - Test stored electronics quarterly
- **Multiple cages** - Don't put all items in one container
- **Document contents** - Label what's inside each cage
- **Include instructions** - How to deploy protected items

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
### **Cost Savings**
- **Military surplus** - Ammo cans often cheaper than new
- **Aluminum foil** - Heavy-duty works for small items
- **Mesh screen** - Hardware cloth cheaper than specialized mesh
- **Group purchases** - Split costs with neighbors

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
### **Deployment Strategy**
- **Wait before opening** - EMP events may have multiple pulses
- **Test environment** - Use sacrificial device first
- **Gradual deployment** - Start with least critical items
- **Keep some protected** - Always maintain emergency reserves

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
### **Trash Can Cage Checklist**
- [ ] Galvanized steel can (magnet test)
- [ ] Clean of all paint/rust
- [ ] Cardboard liner installed
- [ ] Foam padding for protection
- [ ] Aluminum tape for sealing
- [ ] AM radio test passes
- [ ] Contents properly wrapped
- [ ] Inventory list attached

[src: modern_threats/emp/faraday_cage_construction.md]
### **Emergency Cage (10 minutes)**
If you have warning of solar flare:
1. **Aluminum foil** - Heavy duty, multiple layers
2. **Wrap items** - No metal touching foil directly
3. **Seal completely** - No gaps or openings
4. **Place in center** - Away from walls/metal objects
5. **Multiple wraps** - 2-3 layers minimum

This guide covers more advanced first aid topics that are critical in a disaster when professional medical help is unavailable. This is not a substitute for professional medical training.

[src: medicine/advanced_first_aid_guide.md]
In a trauma situation, you must address the most life-threatening injuries first. The MARCH algorithm helps you prioritize:

[src: medicine/advanced_first_aid_guide.md]
* **M - Massive Hemorrhage:** Stop major bleeding. This is the first priority. Apply direct, firm pressure to the wound. If that doesn't work, use a tourniquet.
* **A - Airway:** Ensure the person has a clear airway. If they are unconscious, tilt their head back and lift their chin to open the airway.
* **R - Respirations:** Make sure the person is breathing. If not, begin CPR.
* **C - Circulation:** Check for a pulse. Treat for shock by laying the person down, keeping them warm, and elevating their feet.
* **H - Hypothermia/Head Injury:** Prevent hypothermia by keeping the person warm and dry. Check for signs of a head injury.

[src: medicine/advanced_first_aid_guide.md]
Proper wound care is essential to prevent infection.

[src: medicine/advanced_first_aid_guide.md]
* **Clean the Wound:**
 1. Wash your hands thoroughly with soap and water.
 2. Gently clean the wound with soap and purified water. Do not use hydrogen peroxide or iodine on the wound itself, as this can damage the tissue.
 3. Rinse the wound thoroughly.
* **Dress the Wound:**
 1. Apply a thin layer of antibiotic ointment.
 2. Cover the wound with a sterile dressing.
 3. Change the dressing daily, or more often if it becomes wet or dirty.
* **Monitor for Infection:** Watch for signs of infection, including:
 * Redness and swelling around the wound.
 * Pus or drainage.
 * Increased pain.
 * Fever.

[src: medicine/advanced_first_aid_guide.md]
* **Treatment:**
 * Rest.
 * Drink plenty of fluids.
 * Take over-the-counter fever reducers like acetaminophen or ibuprofen.
* **When to Worry:** A fever accompanied by a stiff neck, severe headache, or confusion can be a sign of a serious infection and requires immediate medical attention.

[src: medicine/advanced_first_aid_guide.md]
* **Treatment:**
 * The biggest danger is dehydration. Drink plenty of fluids, especially water with a small amount of salt and sugar to replace lost electrolytes.
 * Avoid dairy products and fatty foods.
* **When to Worry:** If the diarrhea is bloody, or if it is accompanied by a high fever or severe abdominal pain, seek medical help.

[src: medicine/advanced_first_aid_guide.md]
### Respiratory Infections (Colds and Flu)

[src: medicine/advanced_first_aid_guide.md]
* **Treatment:**
 * Rest.
 * Drink plenty of fluids.
 * Over-the-counter cold and flu medications can help manage symptoms.
* **When to Worry:** If you have difficulty breathing, a persistent high fever, or chest pain, seek medical help.

This guide covers the immediate first aid for sprains and the management of common aches and pains.

[src: medicine/sprains_and_pain_guide.md]
## 1. Treating Sprains: The R.I.C.E. Method

[src: medicine/sprains_and_pain_guide.md]
A sprain is an injury to a ligament (the tissue that connects bones to other bones). The most common location for a sprain is the ankle. For the first 24-48 hours after an injury, follow the R.I.C.E. method to reduce pain and swelling.

[src: medicine/sprains_and_pain_guide.md]
* **Rest:** Stop all activity and rest the injured joint. Do not put weight on it.
* **Ice:** Apply a cold pack (a bag of frozen vegetables works well) to the injured area for 15-20 minutes at a time, every 2-4 hours. Wrap the cold pack in a towel to prevent frostbite.
* **Compression:** Wrap the injured area with an elastic bandage (like an ACE bandage). It should be snug, but not so tight that it cuts off circulation.
* **Elevation:** Keep the injured limb elevated above the level of your heart as much as possible.

[src: medicine/sprains_and_pain_guide.md]
### Things to Avoid for the First 48 Hours (H.A.R.M.)

[src: medicine/sprains_and_pain_guide.md]
* **Heat:** Avoid hot showers, heat packs, or saunas.
* **Alcohol:** Alcohol can increase swelling and mask pain, which can lead to further injury.
* **Running:** Or any other form of strenuous exercise.
* **Massage:** Can increase swelling and bleeding in the injured tissue.

[src: medicine/sprains_and_pain_guide.md]
For common aches and pains (headaches, muscle soreness, etc.), over-the-counter pain relievers can be effective. **Always read the label and do not exceed the recommended dose.**

[src: medicine/sprains_and_pain_guide.md]
* **Acetaminophen (Tylenol):** A good choice for general pain relief.
* **Ibuprofen (Advil, Motrin):** An anti-inflammatory, which makes it particularly good for sprains and other injuries where swelling is a factor.
* **Naproxen (Aleve):** Another anti-inflammatory, similar to ibuprofen.

[src: medicine/sprains_and_pain_guide.md]
**Important Note:** Do not give aspirin to children or teenagers, as it has been linked to a rare but serious condition called Reye's syndrome.

[src: medicine/sprains_and_pain_guide.md]
This guide is for minor injuries. You should seek professional medical help if:

[src: medicine/sprains_and_pain_guide.md]
* You suspect a bone is broken.
* The pain is severe and does not improve with over-the-counter medication.
* The swelling does not go down after 48 hours.
* You cannot put any weight on the injured limb.
* The joint feels unstable or "wobbly."

This guide summarizes the **Run. Hide. Fight.** approach to surviving an active shooter incident. Based on [Ready.gov Active Shooter]( and [DHS guidance](

[src: scenario_specific_plans/active_shooter_response.md]
* **Getting away is the top priority.** Leave belongings behind. Evacuate if safe to do so.
* **Warn others** nearby if you can do so safely.
* **Call 911** once you reach safety.
* **Know exits:** Before emergencies, identify two nearby exits when visiting buildings. Plan escape routes.

[src: scenario_specific_plans/active_shooter_response.md]
* **If you cannot escape safely,** find a secure hiding place out of the shooter's view.
* **Lock and barricade** doors. Turn off lights. Silence electronic devices. Stay quiet.
* **Spread out** - do not hide in groups; make it harder for the shooter to target multiple people.
* **Communicate with police** silently (texts, window signs) if possible.
* **Stay in place** until law enforcement confirms the danger has passed.

[src: scenario_specific_plans/active_shooter_response.md]
* **Last resort** - when in immediate danger and you cannot run or hide.
* **Commit to your actions.** Act aggressively to stop the shooter.
* **Use improvised weapons:** chairs, fire extinguishers, scissors, books - to distract and disarm.

[src: scenario_specific_plans/active_shooter_response.md]
* Familiarize yourself with emergency plans at locations you frequent.
* Participate in active shooter drills.
* Learn first aid and tourniquet training.
* Report suspicious activity to authorities.

[src: scenario_specific_plans/active_shooter_response.md]
* **Keep hands visible and empty** when encountering law enforcement.
* **Follow their instructions** immediately.
* **Seek professional help** to cope with trauma.

Emergencies can occur without warning, and you may not be at home. This guide helps you plan for disasters while traveling between work, home, and other regularly visited locations.

Document the following and keep a copy in your wallet or an accessible location:

[src: scenario_specific_plans/commuter_emergency_plan.md]
* **Normal and alternative routes** to home, work, school, and other frequent destinations.
* **Public transportation options:** Bus, train, ferry - line numbers, stops, fares.

* **Emergency contact list** (on paper, not only on your phone).

[src: scenario_specific_plans/commuter_emergency_plan.md]
* **Identify shelter locations** along your routes (schools, libraries, hospitals, stores).
* **Coordinate with family** on meeting points if you cannot get home.
* **Know your employer's emergency plan** - evacuation, lockdown, communication.

## During the Earthquake: Drop, Cover, and Hold On

[src: scenario_specific_plans/earthquake_plan.md]
* **If you are indoors:**
 * **Drop** to the ground.
 * **Cover** your head and neck with your arms and get under a sturdy table or desk.
 * **Hold On** to your shelter until the shaking stops.
 * Stay away from windows, bookcases, and other heavy objects that could fall.
* **If you are outdoors:**
 * Move to an open area away from buildings, trees, and power lines.
 * Drop to the ground and stay there until the shaking stops.
* **If you are in a car:**
 * Pull over to a clear location, away from bridges and overpasses.
 * Stay in the car with your seatbelt fastened until the shaking stops.

[src: scenario_specific_plans/earthquake_plan.md]
* [ ] **Check for Injuries:** Administer first aid as needed.
* [ ] **Check for Hazards:**
 * Look for fires.
 * Smell for gas. If you smell gas, shut it off at the meter and open the windows.
 * Look for structural damage.
* [ ] **Communicate:**
 * Listen to your emergency radio for instructions from local authorities.
 * Use text messages to contact your out-of-state contact.
* [ ] **Expect Aftershocks:** Be prepared for more shaking. Drop, cover, and hold on.
* [ ] **Help Your Community:** Check on your neighbors, especially those who are elderly or have special needs.

## During Extreme Heat: Stay Cool and Hydrated

[src: scenario_specific_plans/extreme_heat_preparedness.md]
* **Indoors:**
 * Stay in air conditioning when possible. Use fans to improve ventilation.
 * Take cool showers or baths.
 * Avoid using the stove or oven.
* **Outdoors:**
 * Limit outdoor activity to early morning or evening.
 * Wear lightweight, light-colored, loose-fitting clothing.
 * Wear sunscreen and a hat.
 * Drink water frequently. Don't wait until you're thirsty.
* **Know the Signs of Heat Illness:**
 * Heat cramps, heat exhaustion (heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, nausea), heat stroke (high body temp, confusion, loss of consciousness). Heat stroke is a medical emergency-call 911.

[src: scenario_specific_plans/extreme_heat_preparedness.md]
* [ ] **Continue to hydrate** and rest.
* [ ] **Check on vulnerable neighbors.**
* [ ] **Avoid sudden return to strenuous activity.**

This guide provides actionable steps for what to do before, during, and after a flood.

[src: scenario_specific_plans/flood_safety.md]
* [ ] **Know Your Risk:** Check if you live in a flood zone. Visit [FEMA Flood Map Service Center]( to view flood maps.
* [ ] **Create an Emergency Kit:** Water (1 gallon per person per day, 3+ days), non-perishable food, first aid kit, flashlight, batteries, important documents in waterproof container.
* [ ] **Develop a Plan:** Identify evacuation routes to higher ground. Have a family meeting place and out-of-area contact.
* [ ] **Protect Property:** Elevate utilities (furnace, water heater, electrical panel) if in a flood-prone area. Install sump pump with battery backup.
* [ ] **Purchase Flood Insurance:** Standard homeowners insurance does not cover flooding. See [floodsmart.gov](

[src: scenario_specific_plans/flood_safety.md]
* **If flood watch or warning is issued:**
 * Move to higher ground immediately. Do not wait.
 * Avoid walking or driving through floodwaters. Six inches of moving water can knock you down; one foot can sweep away a vehicle.
 * Turn around, don't drown.
* **If you must evacuate:**
 * Take your emergency kit. Shut off gas, electricity, and water if instructed.
 * Follow designated evacuation routes.
* **If trapped in a building:**
 * Go to the highest level. Do not climb into a closed attic.
 * Call 911 if possible and signal for help.
