EMERGENCY KNOWLEDGE CORE - 100 KiB EDITION

PURPOSE
This file is for ordinary people facing serious loss of normal services. It cannot replace trained responders. If reliable emergency services, public-health guidance, evacuation orders, utility instructions, poison control, or professional medical care are available, use them. Under uncertainty, prefer actions that are reversible, simple, and unlikely to create a second casualty.

HOW TO USE THIS FILE
First stop immediate threats. Then stabilize injured people, secure safe water, prevent fire/carbon-monoxide exposure, establish sanitation, shelter from dangerous temperature, account for people, and inventory supplies. Search this file for a problem word: BLEEDING, WATER, FIRE, COLD, HEAT, FOOD, TOILET, POWER, FLOOD, EARTHQUAKE, RADIO, EVACUATION, MEDICINE, REPAIR, GARDEN.

============================================================
1. FIRST TEN MINUTES: DO NOT BECOME THE NEXT CASUALTY
============================================================

LOOK before acting. Hazards that routinely kill rescuers include fire, smoke, toxic gas, carbon monoxide, unstable buildings, energized wires, floodwater, traffic, moving machinery, confined spaces, contaminated chemicals, and violence. Do not enter a dangerous area merely because another person is inside unless you can control the hazard.

Leave immediately for fire, visible structural collapse, strong gas or chemical fumes, rapidly rising water, official evacuation orders, or any situation in which remaining is clearly more dangerous than moving. If you smell gas or suspect a leak, avoid flames and sparks; leave the area and contact the utility/fire service from a safe place if available. Do not operate switches in a suspected gas-filled space.

Treat every downed power line as live. Stay well away; electricity can travel through wet ground, fences, rails, vehicles, and water. If a live wire falls on a vehicle, occupants usually should remain inside unless fire forces exit. If exit is unavoidable, avoid touching vehicle and ground at the same time and move away without taking normal long strides.

Never enter moving floodwater to retrieve property. Water hides holes, debris, contamination, and electrical hazards. Vehicles can float or lose traction in surprisingly shallow moving water. Turn around rather than attempting to cross an uncertain flooded road.

Do not use candles around suspected gas leaks or while fueling. Fire extinguishers are for small, contained fires only when you have a clear escape behind you. If one extinguisher cannot control the fire quickly, leave and close doors behind you if safe.

Count people. Identify children, older adults, pregnant people, people with disabilities, people dependent on refrigerated or powered medical equipment, and anyone missing. Assign simple roles: safety, first aid, water, communications, supplies.

2. LIFE-THREATENING MEDICAL PROBLEMS
============================================================

GENERAL ORDER
1. Make the scene safe.
2. Severe bleeding.
3. Airway and breathing.
4. Circulation/shock.
5. Prevent dangerous heat loss or overheating.
6. Get professional help as soon as available.

SEVERE BLEEDING
Expose the wound if you can do so safely. Apply firm, continuous direct pressure with gauze or clean cloth. Do not repeatedly lift the dressing to look; maintain pressure. If blood soaks through, add material on top and keep pressing.

A deep wound may need wound packing: firmly fill the wound cavity with gauze or clean cloth and continue hard pressure. Do not pack wounds of the chest or abdomen or where an object remains embedded.

For life-threatening bleeding from an arm or leg that direct pressure cannot control, use a commercial tourniquet if available. Place it on the limb above the wound, not across a joint, tighten until severe bleeding stops, record the time, and do not periodically loosen it. Once applied for life-threatening hemorrhage, leave it for medical professionals unless there is an extraordinary circumstance and you have appropriate training.

Do not remove deeply embedded objects. Stabilize them in place and control bleeding around them.

UNRESPONSIVE / NOT BREATHING NORMALLY
Call for help and obtain an AED if available. If an adult or teen is unresponsive and not breathing normally, begin chest compressions in the center of the chest, hard and fast, about 100-120 per minute, allowing recoil. Follow an AED's spoken instructions as soon as one is available. Continue until normal breathing returns, you are relieved, the scene becomes unsafe, or you physically cannot continue. If trained in rescue breathing, use your training; otherwise hands-only CPR is far better than doing nothing for a sudden adult collapse.

CHOKING
If a conscious person can cough or speak, encourage coughing. If they cannot breathe, speak, or cough effectively, use standard choking first aid if you know it and summon help. For infants, pregnant people, or people whose body shape prevents ordinary abdominal thrusts, technique differs; training matters. If they become unresponsive, start CPR and look for a visible object when opening the airway; do not blind-sweep the mouth.

SHOCK
Signs include pale/cool/clammy skin, weakness, confusion, rapid breathing, faintness, or deterioration after injury. Control bleeding, keep the person lying down if tolerated, protect from cold/heat, reassure, and seek evacuation. Do not give food or drink to someone who may need surgery, is severely injured, is vomiting, or has impaired consciousness.

HEAD / NECK / SPINE
After a major fall, crash, diving injury, or blow to the head, minimize unnecessary movement, especially if there is neck/back pain, weakness, numbness, confusion, repeated vomiting, worsening headache, seizure, unequal pupils, or loss of consciousness. Airway and immediate life threats take priority over keeping the spine perfectly still.

BURNS
Stop the burning process. Remove the person from heat/electricity/chemical exposure only when safe. For ordinary thermal burns, cool with clean, cool running water when available; do not use ice directly on significant burns, butter, grease, toothpaste, or powders. Remove rings/watches/tight items before swelling if they are not stuck to skin. Cover loosely with a clean non-stick dressing or clean cloth. Large, deep, electrical, chemical, inhalation, facial, hand, genital, joint, or circumferential burns need urgent professional care.

For dry chemicals, avoid contaminating yourself and brush off dry material before prolonged irrigation unless product-specific guidance says otherwise. For liquid chemicals, remove contaminated clothing and irrigate exposed skin/eyes with lots of clean water while seeking poison-control/emergency guidance. Do not try to neutralize acids with bases or vice versa on the body.

FRACTURES / SPRAINS
If a limb is badly deformed, bone is visible, circulation is impaired, pain is severe, or movement causes major pain, treat as a fracture. Control bleeding. Do not force a deformed limb straight unless circulation is absent and no help is available and you are trained. Immobilize in the position found using padding and a splint that supports joints above and below. Check circulation/sensation before and after splinting. For minor sprains: protect, rest, compression if comfortable, elevation, and short periods of wrapped cold therapy when available.

WOUNDS
Wash hands. Rinse dirty wounds generously with safe water; gently remove visible loose dirt with clean tools if easily accessible. Mild soap may be used around wounds. Do not pour harsh household chemicals into tissue. Cover with a clean dressing and change when wet/dirty. Seek care for bites, deep punctures, crushing injuries, contaminated wounds, wounds with retained foreign material, or spreading redness, swelling, increasing pain, pus, fever, red streaks, or loss of function. Tetanus protection matters when services return.

EYE INJURY
For loose dust, irrigate with clean water. For chemicals, immediate copious irrigation is the priority; continue while seeking urgent help. Do not rub. Do not remove embedded objects. Penetrating eye injuries require shielding without pressure and urgent evacuation.

POISONING
Move away from airborne exposure without exposing yourself. Remove contaminated clothing when safe and isolate it. For ingested unknown substances, do not automatically induce vomiting. Use poison-control/emergency instructions if any communication remains. Keep containers/labels for identification.

3. ILLNESS, DEHYDRATION, AND BASIC CARE
============================================================

DEHYDRATION
Danger signs include inability to keep fluids down, very little urine, marked dizziness/fainting, confusion, lethargy, rapid deterioration, or severe dehydration in infants/older adults. Frequent small sips are often easier than large drinks.

ORAL REHYDRATION SOLUTION (ORS)
When commercial ORS is unavailable, a commonly used emergency mixture is:
1 liter of safe water + 6 level teaspoons sugar + 1/2 level teaspoon salt.
Measure carefully. Too much salt is dangerous. The solution should not taste saltier than tears. Give frequent small amounts, especially after diarrhea/vomiting. Continue breastfeeding infants. Seek medical help for bloody diarrhea, severe abdominal pain, persistent high fever, signs of shock, or severe dehydration.

FEVER / RESPIRATORY ILLNESS
Rest, fluids, and temperature comfort matter. Use over-the-counter medicines only according to their label and individual contraindications. Difficulty breathing, blue/gray lips, new confusion, severe chest pain, inability to stay awake, seizure, or rapidly worsening illness requires urgent care. Separate ill people from vulnerable people when practical, improve ventilation where weather permits, cover coughs, wash hands, and use well-fitting masks when respiratory spread is suspected and masks are available.

MEDICINES
Keep an inventory: person, medicine, strength, dose, schedule, allergies, reason, prescriber/pharmacy if known. Keep medicines dry, secure, and in original labeled containers when possible. Do not casually share prescription drugs. Do not double doses after missed doses unless instructions specifically say so. Insulin and some other medicines have special temperature requirements; preserve cooling without freezing and seek product-specific guidance when communication is available.

PAIN / OTC MEDICINES
Read the active ingredient, not just brand name. Many cold/flu products duplicate acetaminophen or other ingredients and can cause accidental overdose. NSAIDs such as ibuprofen/naproxen may be inappropriate with certain kidney disease, ulcers, bleeding, anticoagulants, severe dehydration, or pregnancy. Aspirin has special risks and should not be given to children/teens with viral illness. When uncertain, non-drug measures and conservative label-directed use are safer than improvising high doses.

4. WATER: FIND, PROTECT, TREAT, RATION INTELLIGENTLY
============================================================

SAFE STORED WATER is best. A common preparedness target is roughly 1 gallon (about 4 L) per person per day for drinking and basic sanitation, with more needed for heat, illness, pregnancy, nursing, strenuous work, and pets.

POSSIBLE HOUSEHOLD SOURCES
Commercial bottled water; clean stored water; melted ice; liquid from food; water from a conventional tank-type water heater if it is not contaminated and the appliance can be isolated safely; water trapped in household pipes. Toilet tank water may be usable only if no chemical cleaner or contamination is present. Do not drink from toilet bowls.

AVOID as drinking sources: water from hydronic heating boilers/radiators, chemically treated pools/spas, floodwater, water with fuel/solvent/chemical contamination, or unknown industrial runoff. Microbial purification does not remove many chemicals, salt, or heavy metals.

TREATMENT
If water is cloudy, let sediment settle and pre-filter through a clean cloth or coffee filter. Boiling is a robust method for biological contamination: bring clear water to a rolling boil for at least 1 minute; at elevations above about 6,500 ft/2,000 m, use 3 minutes. Cool covered in a clean container.

Commercial filters differ. Many backpacking filters remove bacteria/protozoa but not viruses or chemicals. Read the filter's claims; a filter labeled only for particulates is not a purifier. Chemical disinfectants also vary with concentration, temperature, turbidity, and organism. If using household bleach for emergency disinfection, only use plain unscented product suitable for disinfection and follow current emergency-water directions on its label or official instructions for that exact concentration. Do not guess from an old recipe because modern bleach concentrations vary.

If radioactive fallout is the concern, ordinary boiling does not remove radioactive material and can concentrate some contaminants as water evaporates. Use sealed water when possible; particle filtration can reduce some suspended contamination but is not a universal solution.

WATER CONSERVATION
Do not dangerously dehydrate healthy people merely to make supplies last. Instead reduce water used for dishes, laundry, and bathing; use disposable liners/plates when available; cook foods that require less water; capture clean rinse water for non-drinking uses. Mark containers DRINKING, CLEANING, or UNTREATED.

CONTAINER HYGIENE
Use food-grade containers. Keep drinking-water openings clean; pour rather than dipping hands/cups. Store away from fuels, pesticides, and chemicals. If a container previously held toxic chemicals, do not repurpose it for drinking water.

5. SANITATION: STOP DISEASE BEFORE IT STARTS
============================================================

A broken sewer plus unsafe water can cause more casualties than the original event.

HAND HYGIENE
Wash with soap and safe water after toilet/diaper/waste handling; before preparing/eating food; after wound care; and after handling raw animal products. Soap and friction physically remove contamination. Alcohol-based hand sanitizer (at least 60% alcohol) is useful when hands are not visibly dirty but is not a full substitute after some diarrheal illnesses or chemical contamination.

WHEN TOILETS/SEWERS FAIL
First determine whether flushing could cause sewage backup. If authorities say sewer lines are unavailable, stop flushing.

A simple emergency toilet can be made from a sturdy bucket or disabled toilet lined with heavy bags. Add absorbent dry material after use to control liquid/odor. Keep waste closed, away from children/animals, food preparation, and water sources, and follow local disposal instructions as soon as they exist. Separating urine can reduce volume/odor, but do not assume urine or feces is harmless. Human waste can transmit disease; use gloves if available and wash hands.

If burial is the only practical option, keep waste well away from wells, streams, shorelines, flood zones, gardens producing ready-to-eat food, and areas where groundwater is near the surface. Local geology and regulations matter; a universal distance is not safe everywhere.

GARBAGE
Separate sharp objects, human waste, food waste, hazardous chemicals, and ordinary dry trash. Use closed containers to limit insects/rodents. Do not burn plastics, batteries, treated wood, electronics, or unknown chemicals in household fires; toxic smoke can be worse than the trash problem.

DISHES
Scrape food, wash with detergent and the cleanest water practical, then rinse/sanitize as conditions permit. Do not spend scarce drinking water polishing already-clean surfaces while neglecting handwashing and safe food preparation.

6. FOOD: PRESERVE CALORIES WITHOUT CREATING FOOD POISONING
============================================================

FIRST HOURS OF POWER FAILURE
Keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed. As a planning rule, an unopened refrigerator keeps food cold only for a limited number of hours; a full freezer generally holds safe temperature much longer than a half-full one. Time, starting temperature, ambient heat, and door opening matter. If you have a food thermometer, cold perishable food should generally stay at 40 F/4 C or below. When uncertain after prolonged warming, discard high-risk perishables rather than relying on smell or taste. Never taste food to test safety.

High-risk foods include meat, poultry, fish, eggs, dairy, cooked leftovers, cooked rice/pasta, cut melons, sprouts, and many prepared foods. Shelf-stable canned/dry foods are more forgiving if packaging is intact.

CANNED FOOD
Discard cans that are bulging, leaking, badly rusted through, or spurting/foaming abnormally when opened. Do not taste suspicious canned food. Home-canned low-acid food carries particular botulism risk if processing was not validated.

COOKING WITHOUT GRID POWER
The safest default is outdoor combustion. Never use charcoal grills, gasoline engines, or generators indoors, in garages, or near openings where exhaust can enter. Propane/butane/camp stoves should be used only as the manufacturer explicitly allows; many are outdoor-only. Ventilation does not magically make an outdoor appliance safe indoors. Maintain working carbon-monoxide alarms whenever combustion is used near occupied structures.

FOOD PRIORITY
Use foods that will spoil first, then refrigerated shelf-stable items, then long-lived stores. Preserve drinking water and fuel: avoid unnecessarily boiling large water volumes or cooking foods requiring long simmering when resources are scarce.

LONGER TERM PANTRY
Useful staples include rice/grains, oats, pasta, beans/lentils, canned fish/meat/beans/vegetables/fruit, nut butters, shelf-stable milk, oil/fat, salt, sugar, spices, and foods appropriate to dietary/medical needs. Store what people actually eat. Rotate first-in/first-out. Protect dry food from moisture, insects, rodents, heat, and sunlight. Fats and whole grains generally spoil faster than white rice, sugar, salt, or properly dried legumes.

NUTRITION
In short emergencies, calories and safe water dominate. Over weeks/months, include protein, fats, vitamins/minerals, and varied foods. Children, pregnancy, lactation, older age, illness, and heavy labor change needs. Do not rely on one staple indefinitely.

7. FIRE, CARBON MONOXIDE, FUEL, AND AIR
============================================================

CARBON MONOXIDE (CO) is colorless and odorless. Headache, dizziness, nausea, weakness, confusion, chest pain, or multiple people becoming ill in the same enclosed space should trigger immediate suspicion. Move everyone to fresh air and seek emergency care. Do not re-enter until the source is controlled.

GENERATORS
Operate portable generators outdoors, far from doors, windows, vents, crawlspaces, and attached garages, with exhaust directed away from structures. Never run one in a basement, garage, shed attached to living space, or improvised enclosure. Keep it dry without enclosing exhaust. Let hot equipment cool before refueling. Store fuel in approved containers away from living areas and ignition sources.

BACKFEEDING
Never connect a portable generator to household wiring through an improvised male-to-male cord or outlet. This can kill utility workers and occupants and start fires. Proper connection requires a transfer switch/interlock designed for the system and installed according to electrical rules.

CANDLES
Prefer flashlights/LED lanterns. If candles are unavoidable, stabilize them on noncombustible surfaces away from curtains, bedding, children, pets, oxygen equipment, and fuel; never leave unattended.

SMOKE
Get low only if necessary to escape under smoke; close doors behind you. Do not use elevators during a building fire unless authorities specifically direct it. Once safely out, stay out.

AIR QUALITY
Wildfire smoke, dust, demolition, and disease outbreaks require different protections. A well-fitting N95/P100 can reduce inhalation of particles but does not supply oxygen and does not protect against most toxic gases/vapors. Do not enter oxygen-deficient or chemically contaminated spaces based on a particle mask.

8. SHELTER, COLD, HEAT, AND WEATHER
============================================================

SHELTER PRIORITY
Stay out of wind/rain, keep insulation dry, reduce exposed surface area, and isolate bodies from cold ground. One safe room is often easier to heat/cool than an entire home.

COLD
Layer clothing: dry base layer, insulating middle layers, wind/water-resistant outer layer. Cover head/hands/feet without restricting circulation. Replace wet clothing. Use sleeping pads/cardboard/blankets beneath people because ground/floor conduction matters.

HYPOTHERMIA
Watch for intense shivering followed by decreasing shivering, confusion, clumsiness, slurred speech, unusual behavior, drowsiness, or slow breathing. Handle gently, remove wet clothing, insulate, warm the trunk gradually, and seek medical help. Give warm sweet fluids only to an alert person able to swallow. Do not rely on alcohol; it increases heat loss and judgment impairment.

FROSTBITE
Protect from refreezing. Do not rub snow on tissue. If thawing is appropriate and refreezing can be prevented, professional guidance is best. Avoid walking on badly frostbitten feet if alternatives exist.

HEAT
Reduce exertion, seek shade/airflow, drink fluids, cool skin with water/wet cloths, and move vulnerable people to cooler areas. Heat exhaustion can include heavy sweating, weakness, dizziness, headache, nausea. Heat stroke is an emergency: altered mental status, collapse, seizure, or severe overheating requires rapid active cooling and emergency care. Move to shade, remove excess clothing, wet the body and fan, apply cold packs to neck/armpits/groin if available, and arrange evacuation. Do not delay cooling while waiting for transport.

THUNDERSTORMS
If thunder is heard, lightning can strike. Prefer a substantial building or hard-topped vehicle. Avoid isolated trees, exposed ridges, open water, metal fences, and plumbing/wired appliances when lightning is close.

9. POWER AND ELECTRICITY
============================================================

PRESERVE POWER for communications, medical devices, lighting, and information before entertainment. Reduce screen brightness, disable unnecessary radios/apps, and use airplane mode when no network exists while enabling needed local radios periodically.

BATTERIES
Do not short terminals. Keep loose batteries away from coins/keys/metal. Damaged, swollen, hot, hissing, or smoking lithium batteries can ignite; move away from combustibles if safe, do not puncture, and follow fire-service guidance. Charge on nonflammable surfaces and avoid improvised chargers with unknown voltages/polarity.

HOUSE ELECTRICAL
Water plus electricity is dangerous. If flooding reaches outlets/appliances/panel, do not enter standing water to shut power off. Have the utility or qualified person disconnect upstream when possible. After flood damage, wiring/appliances may remain unsafe after drying.

EXTENSION CORDS
Match cord rating to load, keep connections dry, uncoil high-load cords, inspect for damage, avoid running under rugs/doors, and never defeat grounding. Space heaters, hot plates, kettles, and heaters draw large current and can overload cords.

SOLAR
Small panels are most useful for charging batteries/phones/radios. Keep panels oriented toward unobstructed sun. Use compatible charge controllers for storage batteries. Do not connect arbitrary solar-panel voltage directly to lithium cells or electronics.

12 V SYSTEMS
Automotive batteries can deliver enormous current and cause burns/fire. Fuse circuits near the battery positive terminal, observe polarity, ventilate lead-acid batteries during charging, and avoid sparks near batteries. Never use a vehicle engine for power in an enclosed or attached garage because of CO.

10. COMMUNICATION AND INFORMATION
============================================================

Before failure, write important phone numbers, addresses, medications, insurance/policy information, meeting points, and an out-of-area contact on paper.

WHEN CELLULAR IS CONGESTED
Use brief text messages rather than repeated voice calls. Tell recipients: WHO you are, WHERE you are, STATUS, NEED, NEXT ACTION/TIME. Example: "MARTIN FAMILY 4 SAFE AT SCHOOL GYM. NEED INSULIN BY 1800. NEXT CHECK 1600."

Set check-in windows rather than leaving radios/phones constantly transmitting. Conserve battery.

RADIOS
Listen before transmitting. Use plain language. Keep messages short and repeat critical numbers. Record messages with time/source. FRS radios are simple short-range family tools. GMRS and amateur radio have licensing/rules that vary by jurisdiction; emergency rules may differ but do not assume ordinary restrictions disappear. Range depends heavily on terrain, antenna height, buildings, and repeaters; marketing distances are not guarantees.

INFORMATION HYGIENE
Separate observation from rumor. Record source and time: "I saw bridge closed 14:20" is stronger than "someone said all bridges are gone." Corroborate life-changing claims when possible. Do not rebroadcast uncertain casualty counts, contamination claims, evacuation routes, or medical instructions as facts.

COMMUNITY MESSAGE BOARD
Maintain a physical board or notebook with DATE/TIME, PERSON, LOCATION, STATUS, NEED/OFFER, SOURCE, NEXT CHECK. Cross out obsolete entries but preserve history. This works when every network fails.

11. EVACUATION AND NAVIGATION
============================================================

EVACUATE EARLY when ordered or when local conditions are clearly worsening. Leaving before roads become blocked is safer than attempting a heroic late departure. Take people, essential medicines, identification/documents, water, weather-appropriate clothing, communication tools, needed mobility aids, and pets if possible. Property is replaceable.

ROUTES
Know at least two routes and one walking option. Avoid tunnels, low crossings, bridges, coastal roads, wildfire corridors, or landslide slopes when the relevant hazard threatens them. Never choose a shortcut through floodwater.

IF SEPARATED
Use predetermined meeting points: near home, outside neighborhood, and outside region. Leave written notes only where they do not reveal sensitive information to strangers unnecessarily. Include date/time and destination.

MAP BASICS
Keep a paper street/topographic map. Orient it using known landmarks/compass. Mark water, hospitals/clinics, fire/police/emergency shelters, bridges, steep terrain, industrial hazards, and alternate routes. A magnetic compass is more reliable than trying to infer direction from vegetation or folklore.

LOST
Stop, calm down, assess last known location, inventory daylight/weather/water, and avoid wandering if rescuers are likely searching a known area. Signal with visible contrast, whistle, light, radio, or phone when possible. Three repeated signals are widely understood as distress but local practice varies.

12. DISASTER-SPECIFIC FAST RULES
============================================================

EARTHQUAKE
During shaking indoors: Drop, Cover, Hold On. Protect head/neck under sturdy furniture or beside an interior wall away from glass if no cover exists. Do not run through falling debris during strong shaking. Afterward expect aftershocks; leave badly damaged buildings, check gas/electrical/fire hazards, wear shoes/gloves around debris, and do not re-enter structures authorities consider unsafe.

FLOOD
Move to higher ground early. Do not walk, swim, or drive through moving floodwater. Avoid basements when rapid flooding is possible. Floodwater may contain sewage, chemicals, sharp debris, and live electricity. After flood, treat wet electrical/gas systems, soaked medicines, and contaminated food as hazards.

HURRICANE / HIGH WIND
Know evacuation zones. Evacuate surge/flood zones when directed; hiding upstairs cannot reliably defeat storm surge. During extreme wind, shelter in a small interior room away from windows, on the lowest level not threatened by flooding. Remain inside during the apparent calm of an eye until official all-clear.

TORNADO
Move to a basement/storm shelter or small interior room on the lowest safe floor, away from windows. Protect head/neck with arms, helmet, mattress, or sturdy covering. Mobile/manufactured homes are especially vulnerable; use a designated sturdy shelter if time allows.

TSUNAMI
After a strong/long coastal earthquake, sudden sea withdrawal/rise, or official warning, move immediately inland and to high ground; do not wait to watch the water. Multiple waves can arrive over hours; first wave may not be largest.

WILDFIRE
Leave early when ordered. Close windows/doors, take medicines/documents/water, and avoid routes toward smoke/fire. Do not depend on a garden hose or ordinary mask to survive an advancing fire front. N95/P100 masks reduce smoke particles but do not protect from CO or oxygen deficiency.

LANDSLIDE
Move away from the path and toward higher stable ground if debris flow/slide threatens. Be alert near steep slopes, drainage channels, and areas burned by wildfire during heavy rain. Do not cross active debris flows.

NUCLEAR/RADIOLOGICAL FALLOUT
Distance, shielding, and time matter. Get inside a substantial building quickly; basement or central interior provides more shielding than windows/exterior walls. Remove outer clothing carefully and bag it away from people; shower with soap/water if possible without harsh scrubbing. Stay sheltered and follow official radiation guidance when available. Do not take potassium iodide unless public-health/medical authorities recommend it for the specific event; it protects only the thyroid from radioactive iodine, not the rest of the body or other radionuclides.

CHEMICAL RELEASE
Move away from the release and heed official shelter/evacuation instructions. Going "upwind" is not always simple in urban terrain; distance and entering a clean building can matter. Close windows/doors and ventilation if shelter-in-place is ordered. Do not improvise respirators from wet cloth and assume protection from toxic gas.

PANDEMIC / RESPIRATORY OUTBREAK
Stay home when sick if possible, improve ventilation, reduce close crowded exposure, wash hands, use well-fitting masks where appropriate, and protect people at highest risk. Maintain medications and communication with health services. Exact recommendations depend on the pathogen; use current public-health guidance when available.

13. HOME SYSTEMS, GAS, WATER, AND BASIC REPAIR
============================================================

MAIN SHUTOFFS
Know before disaster where water, gas, and electrical main controls are and what tools they require. Label them. Do not shut gas off casually if you do not know how it will be safely restored; many utilities require qualified restart. If gas odor/hissing or obvious line damage exists, leave first and contact the utility/fire service.

WATER LEAK
If a pipe bursts and shutoff is accessible without electrical/structural danger, close the nearest valve or main. Open a lower faucet to relieve pressure if useful. Protect electrical equipment from water only if safe. Temporary patches are temporary; keep pressure low and monitor.

ROOF LEAK
Protect people from ceiling collapse/electrical hazards. Move valuables, catch water, and only access roofs when weather, structure, fall protection, and skill make it reasonably safe. Wet/icy roofs kill people; interior damage is preferable to a fall.

TOOLS WORTH HAVING
Adjustable wrench, pliers, screwdrivers, hammer, utility knife, hand saw, tape measure, duct/repair tape, cord/rope, buckets, work gloves, eye protection, dust masks/respirators appropriate to hazard, headlamp, multimeter for trained users, fasteners, tarps, plastic sheeting, shovel, pry bar, fire extinguisher.

POWER TOOLS
After injury or fatigue, manual tools may be safer. Do not bypass guards. Do not use electrical tools in wet environments unless specifically protected and supplied through appropriate ground-fault protection.

14. COMMUNITY ORGANIZATION AND SECURITY
============================================================

Small groups outperform isolated households when roles and information are organized. Inventory SKILLS as well as supplies: medical, nursing, electrical, plumbing, mechanical, construction, radio, languages, childcare, elder care, cooking, logistics.

Create a daily rhythm:
- safety/weather/infrastructure update;
- headcount and vulnerable-person check;
- water and sanitation status;
- medical needs;
- food/fuel/power inventory;
- tasks and buddy assignments;
- communications/check-in windows;
- written log.

SECURITY
Most useful security is boring: lighting, locks, controlled access, knowing neighbors, not advertising scarce supplies, buddy movement, de-escalation, and clear rules for children/visitors. Avoid creating hazards with improvised traps, electrified barriers, uncontrolled fires, or weapons handling. Preventing conflict preserves resources better than winning one.

RESOURCE SHARING
Record loans/trades. Separate communal emergency reserves from personal property by agreement. Use transparent rationing rules. Give priority to life-critical medical needs, safe water, vulnerable people, and tasks that preserve shared infrastructure. Unexplained favoritism rapidly destroys cooperation.

SANITARY WORK ZONES
Separate clean food/water area from toilet/waste area, fuel/chemical storage, sick-care area, and dirty repair work. Simple zoning prevents cross-contamination.

============================================================
15. CHILDREN, OLDER ADULTS, DISABILITY, PREGNANCY
============================================================

Do not treat every person as a standard adult. Children dehydrate and lose heat faster and may not describe symptoms well. Older adults may have reduced thirst sensation, mobility limits, medication dependence, or heat/cold vulnerability. Mobility, vision, hearing, cognitive, and developmental disabilities require accessible routes and communication, not merely extra supplies.

Keep medication lists, mobility devices, glasses/hearing-aid batteries, charging cables, feeding supplies, continence supplies, and backup communication methods together.

Pregnancy increases the importance of hydration, nutrition, temperature control, infection prevention, and access to obstetric care. Heavy bleeding, severe abdominal pain, seizure, severe headache/vision changes, difficulty breathing, labor concerns, or markedly reduced fetal movement when movement is normally established require urgent obstetric evaluation where available. Do not improvise complex delivery procedures from a short disaster guide if skilled help can be reached.

INFANTS
Breastfeeding, when established and possible, provides nutrition and reduces dependence on clean-water preparation. If formula is used, safe water, clean preparation, correct dilution, and manufacturer instructions are critical; never stretch formula by adding extra water.

16. LONGER-TERM FOOD, GARDENING, AND STORAGE
============================================================

Before planting, secure safe water, sanitation, shelter, and enough near-term calories. Gardens are not instant emergency food.

Prioritize crops suited to climate and season. High-value categories: calorie crops (potato/sweet potato/corn where suitable), legumes for protein/nitrogen fixation, leafy greens for micronutrients, squash/root crops for storage, herbs for flavor. Local extension/agricultural knowledge beats generic planting calendars.

SOIL
Do not garden in soil contaminated by floodwater, industrial chemicals, peeling lead paint, sewage, or ash from burned structures without assessment. Raised beds with known clean soil may reduce risk but do not solve contaminated irrigation or airborne deposition.

COMPOST
Compost ordinary plant material and appropriate food scraps. Keep human feces, pet feces, chemically contaminated material, diseased plants where inappropriate, and unknown ash out of food-garden compost unless using a validated treatment system.

SEEDS
Keep seeds cool, dry, dark, labeled with crop/variety/year. Open-pollinated varieties can generally produce seed that resembles parents more reliably than many hybrids. Seed-saving details vary greatly by species because of cross-pollination and biennial life cycles.

FOOD PRESERVATION
Drying, fermenting, freezing, water-bath canning, and pressure canning are not interchangeable. Low-acid foods require validated pressure-canning procedures to control botulism; do not invent processing times/pressures. If validated instructions are unavailable, use safer preservation methods rather than guess.

============================================================
17. BASIC MECHANICAL AND MATERIAL PRINCIPLES
============================================================

LEVER: force x distance from pivot balances load x load distance. Longer handles increase torque but can break fasteners/tools.

PULLEY: fixed pulleys change direction; multiple supporting rope segments can reduce input force but require more rope travel. Never stand under suspended loads. Ordinary rope/anchors of unknown rating are not safe for lifting people.

FASTENERS
A temporary repair should fail visibly rather than catastrophically. Use multiple independent attachments on overhead/weather-critical loads. Do not rely on adhesive tape alone for structural support.

ROPES/KNOTS
Useful general knots include bowline (fixed loop), figure-eight family (stoppers/loops), clove hitch (temporary attachment), two half hitches (securing), and trucker's hitch (tensioning). Practice before emergency. Knots reduce rope strength; climbing/life safety requires rated equipment and training.

CORROSION
Keep steel dry/oiled, separate dissimilar metals when galvanic corrosion matters, rinse salt contamination with fresh water when possible, and protect electrical connections from moisture.

LUBRICATION
Many mechanical failures are friction/contamination problems. Clean before lubricating; use the correct lubricant when known. Do not put petroleum products on oxygen equipment or where manufacturer forbids them.

18. BASIC SCIENCE AND CALCULATION REFERENCE
============================================================

Metric:
1 liter = 1000 mL.
1 kilogram = 1000 g.
1 m = 100 cm.
Approx: 1 US gallon = 3.785 L; 1 quart = 0.946 L; 1 lb = 0.454 kg; 1 inch = 2.54 cm; 1 mile = 1.609 km.

Temperature:
C = (F-32)*5/9.
F = C*9/5+32.
0 C=32 F; 20 C=68 F; 37 C=98.6 F; 100 C=212 F at sea level (boiling point decreases with altitude).

Electricity:
Power W = volts V x amps A for simple DC/resistive loads.
Energy Wh = watts x hours.
Approx battery runtime hours = usable watt-hours / average watts. Real systems lose energy in conversion and batteries should not always be fully discharged.
Resistors in series add. For simple parallel resistors: 1/Rtotal = 1/R1 + 1/R2 ...
Never use formulas as permission to work on energized mains wiring without training.

Mechanics:
Speed = distance/time.
Density = mass/volume.
Pressure = force/area.
Mechanical power = work/time.

Water mass: 1 L of fresh water is about 1 kg; 1 US gallon about 8.34 lb. Large water containers are heavy: a 55-gallon drum holds roughly 459 lb/208 kg of water, excluding container.

Slope/grade percent = rise/run x 100. A 10% grade rises 10 units per 100 horizontal units.

============================================================
19. RECOVERY: RECORDS, MONEY, AND CONTINUITY
============================================================

Keep a written incident log: date/time, observations, decisions, injuries, medicines, expenses, contacts, photos/file numbers when devices work. This supports insurance, aid, medical continuity, and handoffs.

Preserve identification, insurance, property records, prescriptions, account contacts, and emergency cash in protected copies. Do not expose account/password data on public message boards. If banking networks are unavailable, track every cash/barter/loan transaction on paper.

Photograph damage only when safe and before major cleanup if possible. Do not delay life safety to document property.

When services return, assume scams will appear. Verify contractors, charities, payment requests, and official identities through independent channels when possible. Be cautious of anyone demanding immediate irreversible payment or credentials.

============================================================
20. PREPARATION THAT MULTIPLIES EVERY OTHER SECTION
============================================================

If you are reading this before a disaster, training and organization are higher-value than exotic gear.

Learn CPR/AED and bleeding control. Learn how to shut off household water and identify electrical/gas controls. Test smoke and CO alarms. Practice family evacuation and communication plans. Keep paper contacts/maps. Maintain fire extinguishers. Rotate food/water/medicines. Keep vehicles and batteries maintained. Back up critical records offline. Know neighbors with complementary skills.

A BASIC HOME CACHE
Safe water and containers; shelf-stable food; prescription medicines and medical list; first-aid/bleeding supplies; flashlights/headlamps; batteries/power banks; battery/hand-crank radio if useful; smoke/CO alarms; fire extinguisher; weather clothing/blankets; sanitation bags/buckets/soap; work gloves/eye protection/N95 masks; basic tools; can opener; paper maps/contacts; cash; chargers/cables; pet/infant/disability-specific supplies.

============================================================
21. DECISION RULES UNDER UNCERTAINTY
============================================================

1. Do not create a second casualty.
2. Fix the fastest fatal threat first: unsafe scene, massive bleeding, absent breathing, fire/CO, drowning/flood, lethal temperature.
3. Favor safe water and sanitation before elaborate food plans.
4. Separate clean from dirty: water/food/wounds away from sewage, chemicals, raw food, and contaminated tools.
5. Do not use smell/taste as proof of safety for CO, chemicals, spoiled food, or contaminated water.
6. Avoid invisible hazards: electricity, CO, structural instability, contaminated floodwater.
7. Do not turn a temporary inconvenience into a permanent injury through risky repair, roof work, live electrical work, confined-space entry, or improvised fuel use.
8. Record source and time for important information.
9. Keep redundant ways to communicate, navigate, light, cook, and obtain water.
10. When uncertain about a precise medical/chemical/electrical procedure, conservative stabilization and expert escalation is usually safer than confident improvisation.
