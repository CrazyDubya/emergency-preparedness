# Urban Water Procurement and Purification Guide

In a disaster, clean drinking water is your most immediate priority. This guide outlines how to secure and purify water in an urban environment.

> **Source note:** Emergency-water guidance differs slightly among current authoritative U.S. sources. This guide uses CDC/EPA concentration-aware dosing as the primary operational rule, while retaining the American Red Cross as a secondary reference. Always follow a current product label or local public-health instruction when available.

## 1. Storing Emergency Water

The easiest and safest water is what you store *before* a disaster.

* **How Much:** Store a **minimum** of **1 gallon (4 liters) per person, per day**. Aim for at least a 3-day supply, with a 2-week supply being ideal. Account for pets and increased needs in hot weather, illness, pregnancy, or heavy activity.
* **Best Containers:**
  * Commercially bottled water (easiest, safest).
  * Food-grade water storage containers.
  * Thoroughly cleaned, sturdy, sealable food-grade containers.
* **Sanitizing Your Own Containers:**
  1. Wash the container and rinse thoroughly.
  2. Mix 1 teaspoon of unscented household bleach containing 5%-9% sodium hypochlorite with 1 quart (4 cups) of water.
  3. Pour the solution into the container, close it, and shake so all interior surfaces are touched.
  4. Drain the sanitizing solution and allow the container to air dry or fill with safe water according to current public-health guidance.
* **Storage Location:** Store water in a cool, dark place. Keep containers away from gasoline, pesticides, and other chemicals.

## 2. Finding Water Sources in an Urban Environment

If stored water runs out, potential emergency sources may exist within the home. Their safety depends on what happened to the water system and container.

* **Water-heater tank:** May contain useful water if the building water supply was safe before the event and the tank itself has not been contaminated. Turn off power or fuel before draining and follow the appliance instructions.
* **Plumbing:** Water may remain in pipes if the system has not been contaminated.
* **Ice:** Melt ice made from water that was safe before the emergency.
* **Canned foods:** Liquid from commercially canned fruits and vegetables can supplement fluids.
* **Toilet tank:** Only as an emergency source if it contains no cleaners/chemicals and the water is otherwise believed uncontaminated; treat it before drinking if safety is uncertain.

**External Sources — use with caution:**
* Rainwater collected from clean surfaces
* Rivers, streams, ponds, or other surface water

**DO NOT assume the following can be made safe by ordinary boiling, filters, or bleach:**
* Water contaminated with fuel, toxic chemicals, firefighting foam, heavy metals, radioactive material, or other hazardous substances
* Seawater
* Water from home-heating boilers/radiators
* Water from containers that previously held toxic chemicals

If chemical contamination is known or suspected, use bottled water or another source. **Boiling and household disinfection kill germs; they do not reliably remove toxic chemicals or salt.**

## 3. How to Make Microbiologically Unsafe Water Safer

If local officials issue a water advisory, follow the exact advisory. A **boil-water** advisory, **do-not-drink** advisory, and **do-not-use** advisory are not interchangeable.

### Step 1: Clarify the hazard

If fuel, toxic chemicals, radioactive material, or significant salinity are suspected, do not rely on boiling, ordinary filters, or bleach. Seek another source or specialized authoritative guidance.

### Step 2: Pre-filter cloudy water

If water is cloudy, murky, or contains debris, let it settle and/or filter it through a clean cloth, paper towel, or coffee filter. This improves later treatment but **does not by itself make the water safe**.

### Step 3A: Boiling — preferred for killing germs

1. Bring clear water to a full rolling boil.
2. Boil for **1 minute**.
3. At high elevation, follow current public-health guidance. CDC uses **above 6,500 ft / about 2,000 m** as the point for a **3-minute** boil; EPA currently publishes a **3-minute** rule above **5,000 ft**. If uncertain at high altitude, the longer 3-minute boil is the conservative choice.
4. Let the water cool naturally and store it in a clean, covered container.

### Step 3B: Household bleach — when boiling is not possible

Use only **regular, unscented liquid chlorine bleach** suitable for disinfection. Do not use scented, color-safe, splashless, or products with added cleaners unless the label specifically states they are suitable for water disinfection.

**First choice: follow the bleach label for drinking-water disinfection.**

If the label has no drinking-water instructions and the active ingredient is **5%-9% sodium hypochlorite**, current CDC guidance is:

| Water | Clear water | Cloudy, murky, colored, or very cold water |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 quart / 1 liter | 2 drops | 4 drops |
| 1 gallon | 8 drops (about 0.5 mL) | 16 drops (about 1 mL) |
| 5 gallons | 40 drops (about 2.5 mL) | 80 drops (about 5 mL) |

1. Add the bleach and mix well.
2. Let stand for **at least 30 minutes** before use.
3. Store in a clean, covered container.

**Why older guides sometimes say 16 drops per gallon:** The American Red Cross currently publishes 16 drops/gallon for 5.25%-6% bleach even for clear water. CDC and EPA currently recommend lower concentration-aware doses for clear water and double them for cloudy/colored/very cold water. The Red Cross rule is therefore a more heavily chlorinated emergency treatment, not necessarily evidence that the older guidance was inherently unsafe. For a canonical preparedness system, prefer the current CDC/EPA concentration-aware table and retain the Red Cross rule as documented secondary guidance.

### Water purification tablets

Follow the package instructions exactly. Different products have different contact times and organisms they reliably kill.

### Portable filters

Filter performance depends on certification and pore size. Many portable filters remove parasites and some remove bacteria, but ordinary portable filters generally do **not** remove viruses or dissolved chemicals/salts. Reverse osmosis can remove a wider range of microbes and salts when correctly designed and maintained. Follow the specific device's certification and instructions.
